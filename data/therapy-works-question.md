I have been through therapy twice. Both times, I walked in genuinely wanting an external perspective — not validation. I put in all the efforts. I wasn't pushing back or treating it like a debate. But what I got versus what I actually needed turned out to be very different things.

## First time

I joined a great company straight out of college. I was doing well, leading visible projects, getting good feedback. Then COVID hit. Everything went remote, casual conversations vanished, but expectations somehow went up.
The boundary between work and life blurred, and I found myself working way more than I could sustain. On top of that, there was all that leadership anxiety about *"how do we really know people are working from home?"* — so the pressure to "prove yourself" doubled.

I kept getting more work on an already overflowing plate. Every new request felt like a test of my commitment. As someone barely a year out of college, I was terrified of damaging relationships I'd worked hard to build in the team. For this one project, three months of late nights to hit a customer deadline left me wrecked. And I was looking forward to breathing again, but another "urgent" thing dropped right after, and I said yes to that too.

Somewhere in that stretch, I started getting continuous headaches. Insomnia followed. I was anxious and irritable over small things. My family noticed. I brought it up in team meetings — said we needed buffer time between big projects multiple times — and all I got was: *"After this project, it will all be fine."*


When I realized nobody was going to align with me, I took a couple of days off — my own PTO — to focus on mental health. Looking back, that was my cry for help. And it worked, in a way. One of my sister team's seniors, after seeing my post in team channel, sent me a meeting invite where mental health benefits were introduced. So I signed up for the program my company offered. I was genuinely worried about burning out and leaving a job I otherwise very much liked and enjoyed.

Over the next three to four months, I attended my therapy sessions very diligently, doing all the homework and being honest about what was going on. And the conclusion from therapy was simple: I need to be more assertive. In those sessions, I was told I have a good sense of what's right, but I don't give myself permission to act on it because I'm too worried about how it affects others. So: communicate needs, set boundaries, be assertive.

That worked. For about five years, it worked. But recently, I've started noticing that all that had made my way of working to be too rigid and it is no longer the correct tool to be able to achieve what I want to. Now that I'm a lead, my words carry five times more weight. And that same assertiveness has led people early in their careers to avoid some awkward conversations - and the feedback, which should have come to me directly, now reaches me through other channels. I have been applying that very same tool I was given in those therapy sessions in leadership too, but tool is not generic.

### Approaching work differently

Looking back, I think the problem was that I was treated as an individual with a problem, not as a person stuck in a system that was actually broken. The therapy fixed me — my inability to say no, my need of people pleasing — but it never touched the relationship between me and my work environment. It gave me a personal coping strategy when what I really needed was someone to help me function inside a team where the dynamics were the real issue.

This time, instead of going back to therapy, I found a mentor. One hour with him gave me more clarity than those months of sessions. He asked me to do something I'd never considered: understand that it's a blessing to get this feedback so early on in my leadership journey. And that if I already struggle with understanding why my statements land with people the way they do, then I should start with *faking* the empathy part during technical discussions for the first initial weeks, and then actively spend time actually starting to feel that way, putting myself in others' shoes. It needed to be an act in the beginning for I already struggle with that at workplace, given my habits of calculating people's responses from therapy.

For me, this is a major shift — because therapy had essentially told me that everyone else was wrong and I was right to worry about just myself. My mentor has told me that there are situations when you need to do that, but most of the other situations you have a way out, even by caring for everyone. To stretch into someone else's perspective, even if I had to pretend at first. It's a long 5-6 month process, and I need to be honest about it. But I already feel more in control and understanding of what I'm doing.

## Second time

I started dating someone I had known for years. Pretty quickly, I realized we had a massive gap in how we handled conflict. Her reaction to an uncomfortable situation was to make up a lie or give a false commitment — just to soothe things in the moment, hoping it would never come up again. Of course, it always came up again. Once that happened a couple of times, my instinct was to resort to a full-on interrogation, maybe trying to confirm if I was being lied to this time or not. I never realized I was overwhelming her with this distrust, triggered by my overthinking. And she would make up another lie to kill the fight. The loop went on and on.

I went to therapy again. I explained everything I disagreed with, how I felt like there was one "correct" way to react, and how that didn't match how I processed things with her, and how I see no middle ground between us.
But the conversation kept circling back to making me feel good about my side of the story — how I didn't had these issues in past dating experiences, how my sense of how relationships should work aligns with what therapists research has concluded.
Now I realize I already knew the gaps in her ideas, thanks to all the time I was spending in my overthinking of everything that she would tell me, trying to see if it contradicted something else she had said to me earlier. I was blind to mine, because she would rather avoid telling me them.

I expected therapy to help me discover those. Instead, the major focus was: *"You've told her about your boundaries and how you don't feel respected, and she's not honouring them, so you should have a talk about this."* And that talk would push her into another lie, and the cycle would start again. It felt like I hadn't hired a therapist; I had hired an advocate for my side of a legal battle. At no point did my therapist discuss what I was doing wrong, or how I was being unfair to this girl.

The relationship ended soon after. We were deeply incompatible. Therapy didn't end it, but it also didn't help me see my own part in the mess.

### Approaching relationships differently

Now I realize the real issue wasn't the topics we were fighting about — it was that we just didn't know why the other person was the way they were, because we didn't use our time understanding that part of each other.

Only now I have someone who is making me start to realize that one of my biggest blind spots in relationships has been patience. I know only now that every partner needs to understand the pattern of why the person in front of them is the way they are. Almost like an origin story of their partner.
I lacked that empathy towards every partner. Because even though I knew their childhood, I had no idea why they would get annoyed at certain topics, why their reaction to certain small things was such a knee jerk response. I assumed that knowing them as a friend for years meant knowing them as a partner. That was false.

## Therapy works?

I've started noticing a pattern. Therapists have this incredibly sophisticated vocabulary now for toxic relationships — boundaries, trauma, narcissism, gaslighting — and they're very good at spotting when something is unhealthy. But in my experience, they often seem less equipped to help people stay in difficult but meaningful connections.

There's this reflex where any serious stress gets read as evidence that a relationship is broken, when honestly, even healthy relationships generate strain. Loving people disappoint you. Friends misunderstand you. Family stresses you out. If the goal is to eliminate all of that, you're not fixing anything — you're just learning to walk away. Walking away solves one problem, but it doesn't teach you how to tolerate disappointment, repair something, or take responsibility for your own part in a mess.

Therapy can be a good option if you're in freefall and need something to grab onto. It catches you. But if you never take the cast off, your muscles atrophy. You forget how to walk normally. You start protecting the leg even after it's healed.

I don't need an echo chamber when I go to therapy. I need someone who can help me understand my own blind spots. They don't need to be just a therapist to do that. Over the last year, I've gotten better at finding those people.

I'm currently in similar situations as before, but I don't feel lost this time. I've found my people. It's messier than having a framework handed to you, but somehow, I feel more in control. The cast is coming off, and I'm relearning how to move.

## What made me think of this?

I watched a movie called "The Invite" recently. In the climax, the therapist tells a couple to split, but by the end, you realize the relationship wasn't dead — they just lost the tools to understand what the other person felt. All they needed was a fight, all the memories of the good times, and a simple piano.
