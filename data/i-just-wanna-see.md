## You ready for that new thing in career?

Applying this "I just wanna see" mindset to low-stakes hobbies is one thing. But taking it into your career — where performance reviews, politics, and livelihoods collide — is where it really gets tested. When expectations are messy and feedback is harsh, the default instinct is to get defensive. But when you treat work as an experiment in extreme ownership, the entire game shifts.

https://www.fastcompany.com/91565069/why-saying-yes-early-in-your-career-pays-off-later?utm_source=firefox-newtab-en-intl

I’ve seen this play out very clearly in my career.
I was a junior engineer, and in my first few months I got openly blamed by my manager for “not performing well enough.” Missed expectations, unclear updates, work that technically got done but didn’t inspire confidence. I, like a lot of people in that position, started to write off the manager as unfair, blamed the lack of proper onboarding, or mentally checked out. The manager I had before this one was better you know?

Until I realized that if I let things go their way I am just not testing my own limits of what I can do. And this is not a career advice, but I felt the ceiling of what I can learn in that org was higher than what I would get anywhere in any team or any company in India (and me, the Senior engineers from my then team and even Principal engineers from then team wholeheartedly agree to this even now, that we've done the best work of our career in that team).
So I took it painfully personally. I stopped arguing in my 1:1s. I didn’t defend himself. I went back and asked a harder question: “If this keeps happening, what part of this is actually under my control?”
I realized the problem wasn’t just code quality. It was communication. My manager couldn’t see progress early enough. My design thinking wasn’t explicit. I ssumed silence meant approval. So even if everything that was given to me was getting shipped on time and with great quality, my manager just assumed that it's the easy stuff.
So I changed everything I could control.
I started over-communicating. Daily updates, even when nothing was “done.” I wrote short one pager designs before implementing. I asked for feedback early, sometimes uncomfortably early. I practiced explaining trade-offs instead of just shipping code. I made sure my manager never had to guess what I was doing or why.
Six months later, I was effectively operating at a senior level.
Not because I suddenly became 10x smarter, but because I eliminated ambiguity around my work. Other engineers looped me in their projects. Even though I was 3 years out of college I was leading a cross-org project on my own and leading a group of 8 engineers. I became the person people went to because I reduced uncertainty, not because that I wrote the fanciest code.
It's a different story how all that was still not enough to convince my then manager to see the progress, and I left the company soon after. But all that came in handy when I joined my current company, the ownership and accoutability I showed from the first day was what made me trustable in a new team where I had no rapport. I got an opportunity to lead a multi-quarter, multi-engineer effort and that ultimately led to my promotion to next level too.
And all this surprised me too. What stood out to me was how fast it happened once I internalized responsibility. The blame focused me. I treated feedback, even poorly delivered feedback (full props to me then manager for always speaking in riddles and always giving feedback as a shit-sandwich), as signal.
That giant locus of control is a cheat code. You don’t waste energy arguing about fairness. You spend it upgrading your inputs, your communication, and your decision-making. And over time, people stop blaming you not because they’re nicer, but because there’s nothing left to blame.

Just in the light of transparency, my fast growth and promotion, and very quick gain of visibility in leadership did led to a bunch of issues with a few selective people in my then team. There were people who didn't like the fact that I joined the new company and got promoted earlier than them before they could. There were people who didn't like that I was so protective of my time outside of work and I still finished all the work. There were people who didn't like the fact that whenever I would see something wrong, and I was present in a meeting, I would speak up, they would gossip how I am looking for validation from leadership. Fully unknown to the fact that people in this very leader, leadership were the ones who asked me to fix those things when they come up. I was also very lucky because a lot of these gossip turned feedback turned complaints reached my skip manager, and he pulled me in a call and told me to keep working like I am without getting disturbed by these things but also try to give these "immature" (my words) people some comfortable lies so that they don't create noise and disturb my work.

## Wasn't as easy as I say

Reading through all of this — taking extreme ownership, treating embarrassment as a sport, and adopting personas — it might sound like a bulletproof mindset I just woke up with one day. The truth is, getting to a place where I could even *think* like this required unlearning years of self-doubt. The anger I used to rebuild my life didn't come out of nowhere; it was the result of a long timeline of being made to feel small.

School: Kanjoos. A guy said baap ne aankhon mein bhi kanjoosi kar di.
I outgrew the friendship, couldn't fit in anymore they weren't unlearning the shit we conditioned to and was flexing their ignorance.

Coaching city: memes used to get shared about me clicking pictures without my knowledge. Someone mentioned it to a friend of mine. I would ask a lot of questions during class (because the professor wouldn't be available post class - he was one of the reasons why everything in my career worked perfectly) and people would call me question-mark loudly in class. For me it was important to clear my entrance exams knowing fully well the situation at home and I felt if I'm paying lakhs for them to teach me, then I should actually know things.

College: Uncool. Shut door to my face during exams, just my anxiety during exams. Until third year when two friends I made told me why I am the way I am - anxiety and walking during exams, thinking I don't deserve good things.

First year out of college: Got into therapy for the first time in my life, felt like I am being asked to adjust by everyone and still not getting my due. Got told that I need to be more assertive. Created ruckus when I asked for off to focus on mental health.

Now it's more like we don't need to like each other to work together. You can obviously find better people than me to mentor you, I can definitely find other people to keep in my projects. I will fight for you outside of our 1:1 setting but I need to know we're on the same page of the quality and speed.
