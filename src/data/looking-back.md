Well, I have some good news: I got promoted recently.

Later that evening, my skip-level manager called me. He said, "Take a moment and really think about this. Right now, it might not feel like a big deal, but down the line, you’ll realize just how precious these milestones are. Do something today that makes you remember this moment."

That conversation is the reason I’m writing this. I want to anchor this day by looking back on everything that’s happened over the last 6.5 years of my career.

Talking to him also sparked a sobering realization: in the best-case scenario, if I work until I’m 60, I’ll probably only see about two more promotions over the next 32 years. Think about that math for a second. I hit two promotions in the last 6.5 years — and the next two could take another three decades.

The other push to write this is just how violently fast industries are shifting with AI. Honestly, I don’t even know how many of those 32 years I’ll actually spend working in this field.

## The Baseline: Where It All Started

Looking back, my career kicked off in 2019 at a tech giant — one of the largest companies in the world. Back then, I had this relentless internal drive to build things, ship things, and learn everything I could. Luckily, I landed on a product with endless work to do.

The first real roadblock, though, was my own attitude. Up until that point, I had never really collaborated with people. Throughout school and college group projects, I was either the lone wolf driving everything from scratch or someone who showed up at the tail end to clean things up. Suddenly, I was in an environment where you had to work shoulder-to-shoulder with people from day one to the finish line.

I was completely unprepared for that dynamic. The funny — and frustrating — thing about people is that self-interest almost always wins. People naturally want to do the least work for the most reward (or pull off both at once). When you work alongside people like that — and believe me, you’ll run into them everywhere — it drains you completely. You gradually absorb the cynical lesson that doing good work doesn’t guarantee rewards. In fact, you often pay a penalty for being reliable: while others skate by, all the extra work just piles onto your plate.

Inevitably, I came close to burning out. I started therapy, and the biggest takeaway was simple: I needed to be more assertive at work.

It helped, but it also meant stepping on plenty of toes. I started being very vocal about what I was delivering, calling out people who took unearned credit, and making it blatantly clear what I expected in return for my contributions. That friction cost me — I got passed over for promotion a couple of times.

Still, I genuinely liked the work, so burnout wasn’t why I left. What finally pushed me out was how the company handled the 2023 layoffs.

They announced they’d be cutting X% of the global workforce over the following three months. The catch? They wouldn’t announce who was impacted upfront — instead, names would drop on a rolling basis. Instead of just X% of people worrying, 100% of the company lived in perpetual anxiety for months. To make matters worse, the cuts bled way past that three-month window, with leadership framing it as the "new normal" for years to come. Promotions were shelved under the guise of "raised bars," annual raises were laughable, and equity refreshes for employees hitting their four-year cliffs were scrapped entirely.

By the time I left, the pay gap between me — someone with 4.5 years of tribal knowledge, domain expertise, and internal context — and a fresh college grad was a measly 4 Lakhs. That made my decision clear. I had zero confidence I could grow there anymore, and with the rolling cuts hanging over everyone’s heads, nobody even knew if they’d have a job tomorrow.

## The First Learning: Choose a Good Manager Over Everything

That’s when I joined my current company. Right from the jump, I knew I wouldn’t be dealing with the same old baggage. Sure, I’d have an entirely new set of problems to tackle — but new problems are always better than old, stagnant ones.

Before joining, I had an introductory chat with my skip-level manager that stretched over two hours, split across two days. That was my first clue that I was dealing with someone exceptional. Why would a senior leader spend hours answering endless questions from a prospective hire just to get him on board? My cynical first thought was, *Are they desperate? Is this a red flag?* But looking back, it was simply an honest preview of who he is as a leader.

That initial dynamic has held up over the past two and a half years. I’m still completely unfiltered with him — borderline blunt whenever I feel unheard. And like any great leader, he cuts through the noise, grounds the situation, and helps me focus on what actually matters.

Because of that mutual trust, he hands me the ambiguous, high-stakes problems that others might shy away from, and I make it my personal responsibility to deliver every single time. In return, he makes it a point that I get credited and rewarded, even if that means going to bat against his own bosses.

Which brings me to my first major career takeaway: great managers are exceptionally rare. A great manager makes your work life ten times more rewarding. Always prioritize who you’ll be working for over brand names or minor perks. Grill them during your interviews. Ask as many uncomfortable questions as you can, because picking the right manager is easily one of the most consequential decisions you’ll make.

## The Second Learning: There’s More Than One Way to Get There

After feeling pushed around at my first job — where I constantly watched others take credit for work I broke my back on — I developed a sharp, no-nonsense communication style. I became radically transparent about my opinions.

To me, it was never personal. I don’t harbor grudges against anyone I’ve worked with; at most, I’m just indifferent. My intent was never to alienate people, but to ruthlessly pressure-test ideas so we could ship the best possible product. Over time, though, I learned that most people lack the emotional detachment to separate their ego from their ideas. When you challenge their proposal, they take it as a personal attack. I was wired to see that distinction clearly, but most people aren’t. Everyone — including my skip — kept advising me to "massage egos" and play politics. I refused, and I still got promoted.

*(A quick footnote from September 2026: In the early years, being blunt didn’t hurt me, largely because I was lucky enough to have understanding teammates. But post-promotion, it did start creating friction. People began perceiving me as an intimidating, overpowering figure. So my stance has evolved: you eventually have to learn how to meet people where they are. Don’t take my brute-force honesty as a universal playbook.)*

Another piece of conventional advice people kept feeding me was to write more code — because "code output is the only metric leadership notices." I found that hilarious and deeply backward. Looking at how fast AI was advancing, I assumed these models would easily outpace human coding speed within a few years. Doubling down on being a fast code-monkey felt like a dead end in the long run.

Instead, I intentionally gravitated toward projects that demanded systems thinking, product intuition, direct customer conversations, and figuring things out on the fly. More importantly, I learned how to influence cross-functional peers I had zero authority over (and who often held formal authority over me).

Fortunately, that bet paid off. I got promoted ahead of the typical curve despite writing comparatively little code. Why? Because I owned problems that leadership actually cared about and lost sleep over, keeping my impact front and center.

That’s my second takeaway: there isn’t a single prescribed formula to get to where you want to go. There’s no single personality type or corporate dogma you must adopt to move from point A to point B.

## The Third Learning: Be a Developer, Not Just an Engineer

Regardless of who you are outside of work, your career will inevitably require you to adopt traits that feel foreign to your natural personality. Say you’re naturally introverted or quiet. If you sit silently in high-stakes meetings, your silence becomes a default rubber stamp for whatever is decided — and those blind approvals will eventually come back to haunt you. You have to find your voice in the room.

The same applies to showcasing your work. Hoping people will magically notice your quiet brilliance never scales. You have to learn how to articulate what you’ve built, explain why it matters, and advocate for yourself.

Early on, I dismissed all of that. I thought building slide decks and refining narratives wasn’t "real work." I wanted to focus on technical engineering and assumed everything else would fall into place. That naive illusion shattered at my first company when I had to present a critical post-mortem on a major incident to senior leadership. I was so terrified that my throat closed up — I genuinely sounded like I was about to burst into tears. Every second of that thirty-minute meeting felt like an eternity. The memory still makes me wince.

That ordeal taught me a clear distinction between an engineer and a developer. An engineer focuses narrowly on the craft of building. But being a *developer* means understanding that no single tool defines your job. You have to wear whatever hat the problem demands. Code is just one tool in your belt. Oddly enough, it was watching Carl Sagan in *Cosmos* that taught me how to distill complex ideas into clean visuals and simple, evocative language for leadership. Never discount a skill just because it doesn’t look like code.

This distinction is even more urgent today. We have a wave of fresh graduates entering the tech industry with superintelligent AI models at their fingertips, asking: *Why bother learning deeply if AI can already do it faster and better?*

The answer is **taste**.

AI doesn’t have taste. AI is powered by data, and data can only ever look in the rearview mirror. Creativity looks ahead.

Taste is knowing how to read the room and frame a problem so an executive actually listens. Taste is empathizing with the subtle, unspoken frustrations of real users clicking through your product. You can’t prompt-engineer that instinct — at least not yet — because the people on the other side of the screen are still human beings.

Taste is personal, and it only develops when you actively hone it through trial, error, and reflection. I use AI daily as a sounding board to poke holes in my logic, but only *after* I’ve formed an intuition and thought through the edge cases myself.

## Was Any of This Easy?

Of course not.

From the outside, I made it look effortless. I kept up with all my hobbies, barely missed a gym session, took long evening walks with music, protected my sleep, and rarely let my calendar exceed three meetings a day — all while shipping major projects well ahead of schedule.

Projecting that illusion of effortless execution ended up sparking its own share of workplace politics and friction. But that’s a story for another note, because the situation is still developing, and I’m still figuring out how to navigate it.
